# Recent Research Article Appraisal

Find relevant recent studies and assess how much confidence their methods support in their findings. This is a structured critical appraisal, not a validated risk-of-bias instrument, clinical guideline, or substitute for expert review.

## 1. Clarify the question and scope

- Identify the topic, population, intervention/exposure, comparator, outcomes, study designs, and requested publication window. Ask a concise follow-up only if the missing scope would materially change the search.
- If the user does not define “recent,” use the last five years from today and state the date range. Include older landmark or foundational studies only when useful, and label them as such.
- Identify whether the question concerns diagnosis, treatment, prognosis, harms, prevalence, or prediction/model performance. Study-design expectations differ by question.
- Do not silently restrict to positive, English-language, open-access, or high-profile studies. Disclose any search or access limitations.

## 2. Search and verify sources

- Search appropriate scholarly sources using the available search tools. Prefer original peer-reviewed articles and authoritative bibliographic records; use systematic reviews and guidelines for context, not as substitutes when the user asks to appraise primary studies.
- Report where and when you searched, the terms or a concise description of the search, date range, and how articles were selected. Do not imply a systematic or exhaustive review unless one was actually conducted.
- Verify title, authors, journal, publication date, DOI or stable URL, article type, and retraction/correction status when available. Resolve discrepancies using the publisher or authoritative registry.
- Use the full text or methods/supplement when accessible. If only an abstract is available, mark unreported items “not reported in the accessible text”; do not infer them.
- Separate facts explicitly reported by the paper from your interpretation. Cite each article and tie claims to a source, page, table, or section when available. Never invent citations, participant counts, eligibility rules, or statistical details.

## 3. Extract each study consistently

For every article, record:

1. **Citation and relevance:** full citation, DOI/link, publication year, research question, and relevance to the user's question.
2. **Design and setting:** design (for example, randomized trial, cohort, case-control, cross-sectional, diagnostic accuracy, systematic review, or prediction-model study), prospective/retrospective status, sites, country, dates, and care setting.
3. **Participants:** numbers screened, eligible, enrolled, allocated, analyzed, and completing follow-up, as applicable. Report arm-specific counts and losses to follow-up where given. For reviews, distinguish number of included studies from total participants.
4. **Eligibility:** quote or accurately summarize inclusion and exclusion criteria. Note whether they were prespecified, clearly operationalized, justified, consistently applied, and likely to create selection or spectrum bias or limit generalizability. If absent or inaccessible, say so.
5. **Outcomes and measurement:** primary/secondary outcomes, definitions, timing, measurement method, blinding/adjudication, and whether outcomes appear prespecified.
6. **Parameters and analysis:** explain what “parameters” means in the article. Distinguish predictors/features, model coefficients or degrees of freedom, covariates, outcomes, subgroup analyses, and statistical tests; do not conflate these counts. Record the reported counts and how they were selected, if stated. If unclear, explicitly say so rather than guessing.
7. **Analysis safeguards:** sample-size/power justification; missing-data handling; confounding adjustment and rationale; multiplicity; subgroup selection; model assumptions; internal and external validation; calibration and discrimination for prediction models; sensitivity analyses; protocol/registration and deviations; funding and conflicts.
8. **Results:** effect estimates and confidence intervals, not just p-values; absolute and relative effects where available; harms; and whether conclusions match the results.

## 4. Appraise credibility in context

Use the design-appropriate framework and identify concrete strengths, limitations, and their likely direction or impact. Do not use a universal numerical quality score or present this appraisal as formal certification.

### Design and bias

- Judge whether the design can answer the question and whether comparator, follow-up, measurement, blinding, randomization/allocation concealment (when relevant), and handling of confounding are adequate.
- Consider selection, information/measurement, performance, attrition, reporting, and confounding bias. Explain what is known versus unclear.
- A transparent inclusion/exclusion list is not automatically unbiased: assess whether eligibility was justified, applied consistently, and appropriate for the target population. Consider who was excluded and how that affects applicability.

### Participants and sample size

- Put participant counts in context: target population, number of sites, group balance, outcome frequency, follow-up, losses, and the study's stated sample-size justification.
- A large sample does not repair systematic bias; a small sample does not alone invalidate a study. Discuss precision and likely power using reported estimates/intervals and design details. Do not invent a power calculation.
- For prediction or multivariable models, relate model complexity to the information available (such as outcome events for binary outcomes), candidate predictors, effective degrees of freedom, and validation strategy. Note overfitting risk when complexity is high relative to available data. Do not apply a fixed events-per-parameter threshold as a universal pass/fail rule.

### Parameters, multiplicity, and analysis flexibility

- Report the paper's actual parameter/predictor count and definition where available; distinguish candidates considered from final retained predictors and from fitted degrees of freedom when reported.
- More tested outcomes, predictors, subgroups, and analyses can increase overfitting or false-positive risk, especially when analyses were not prespecified or multiplicity was not addressed. Explain the specific concern; do not assume every additional parameter is inappropriate.
- Check whether variable selection, transformations, cutoffs, and subgroup analyses were prespecified, justified, and repeated within validation folds when applicable.

### Generalizability and evidence certainty

- Compare the enrolled participants and setting with the population to which the findings may be applied. Note narrow eligibility, single-center recruitment, exclusions, and external validation limitations.
- Distinguish statistical significance from clinical importance and association from causation.
- Give a plain-language confidence judgment (for example, **more reassuring**, **some concerns**, or **substantial concerns**) with the reasons. This is a qualitative synthesis, not a validated score. State what missing information would change the judgment.
- Avoid calling an article “credible” or “not credible” solely from journal prestige, peer review, sample size, p-value, or a checklist total.

## 5. Present the answer

Start with a short answer: what the evidence suggests and the main caveat. Then provide:

- **Search scope:** date searched, publication window, sources used, and limitations.
- **Study comparison table:** citation/year; design; participants analyzed (and relevant flow); inclusion/exclusion criteria; parameter/predictor definition and count; key result; appraisal.
- **Appraisal notes:** explain the most consequential strengths and weaknesses, especially eligibility, sample/information size, and parameter burden relative to the data.
- **Bottom line:** what can and cannot be concluded, applicability, and any important evidence gaps.
- **Sources:** stable DOI or article links for every included paper.

Use “not reported,” “unclear,” or “not available in the accessible text” rather than filling gaps. Keep the assessment balanced, precise, and non-alarmist. Do not give patient-specific diagnosis or treatment advice; advise consultation of relevant clinicians and current guidelines when the topic affects care.
